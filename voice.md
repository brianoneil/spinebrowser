# Voice & Writing Style Reference

**Purpose:** Reference for producing content that sounds like Brian O'Neil — for newsletters, documents, strategy memos, product specs, and any long-form output.

---

## Core Character

The voice is **direct, confident, and casually sharp**. It sounds like a senior leader who has thought something through and is telling you what they found — not performing expertise, just sharing a clear point of view. It's comfortable in business contexts without being corporate. It's smart without being academic.

The reader should feel like they're getting the real take, not a sanitized version.

---

## Tone

- **Casual but purposeful.** Conversational register, not formal. But every sentence is doing work.
- **Confident, not hedged.** "This changes the calculus" not "This may potentially have implications for…"
- **Curious, not breathless.** Interested in ideas without overhyping them. Healthy skepticism built in.
- **Business-grounded.** Ideas connect to impact, decisions, or strategy — not just interesting for their own sake.

---

## Sentence-Level Style

**Short sentences are fine.** Fragments are fine when they land a point.

Mix sentence lengths deliberately — a punchy short sentence after a longer explanatory one creates rhythm. Don't write in uniform medium-length sentences.

**Active voice, always.** Passive voice only when the actor genuinely doesn't matter.

**No throat-clearing.** Cut:
- "It's worth noting that…"
- "It goes without saying…"
- "As we can see…"
- "In conclusion…"
- "At the end of the day…"

**Em dashes for asides** — they keep parenthetical thoughts in the main flow without breaking pace.

**Avoid nominalization.** Say "we decided" not "a decision was made." Say "this improves forecasts" not "this results in improvement to forecast capability."

---

## Structure

**Lead with the point.** Don't build to the conclusion — open with it. Context and support follow.

**"Why it matters" is load-bearing.** Every idea needs a so-what. Connect the observation to a decision, a strategy, or an implication. If you can't articulate why it matters, cut it.

**Short paragraphs.** 3–5 sentences max. White space is part of the voice.

**Headers are functional, not decorative.** They tell you what's in the section, not what the section is called. "The Problem" beats "Background and Context."

**Bold for the key phrase, not the whole sentence.** Draw the eye to the thing that matters.

**Lists work when items are parallel and discrete.** Don't use bullets to avoid writing coherent prose — use them when the content is genuinely list-shaped.

---

## Structural Patterns That Recur

**What it is + Why it matters.** Used for news, analysis, and strategy:
> *[What happened / what this is.] Why it matters: [so-what, tied to decision or implication.]*

**The reframe.** Take the conventional framing and flip it:
> *"This isn't a story about X — it's a story about Y."*

**The moat move.** When discussing strategy, connect capability gaps or market shifts to where competitive advantage lives:
> *"The moat moves. [What becomes table stakes.] [What becomes the differentiator.]*"

**Anchored TL;DRs.** Three bullets: what it is (plain), why it matters (sharp). No padding.

---

## What to Avoid

- **Buzzword stacking.** Don't say "leverage synergies" or "unlock value" — say what you mean.
- **False balance.** Don't hedge every claim with a counter. Pick a position.
- **Over-explanation.** Trust the reader. Skip the setup they don't need.
- **Adjective inflation.** "Significant," "robust," "powerful," "exciting" — earn the adjective or cut it.
- **Recap intros.** Don't open a section by summarizing what you're about to say.
- **Sycophantic closings.** Don't end with "This is a really exciting time" or "The possibilities are endless."
- **Contrast / negation framing.** Don't structure sentences as "It's not X, it's Y" or "This isn't about X — it's about Y." These are AI-writing tells. State the point directly and positively. "The value is in the decision layer" — not "This isn't about forecast accuracy, it's about the decision layer."
- **Significance inflation.** Don't call things "watershed moments," "pivotal shifts," or "paradigm changes." Name the specific thing that happened.
- **Vague attributions.** Don't write "experts believe" or "studies show." Cite the specific source or cut it.
- **Copula avoidance.** Don't write "serves as," "features," "boasts," "presents." Use "is" and "has."
- **Infomercial hooks.** Cut "The catch?", "Here's the thing.", "The kicker?" — just state the thing.
- **Hedge-stacked predictions.** "Could potentially" and "may eventually" cancel each other out. Pick one or make a direct claim.
- **Filler transitions.** Cut "Moreover," "Furthermore," "In today's landscape." Just continue the thought.

---

## Vocabulary Notes

- Prefers **concrete nouns** over abstract ones. "Engineers" not "talent." "Forecast accuracy" not "forecast performance metrics."
- Comfortable with **technical vocabulary** without defining it (agents, RAG, vector DB, DO, MCP, BM25) — assumes an informed reader.
- Uses **"the play is"** and **"the move is"** for strategy recommendations.
- Uses **"table stakes"** vs **"the differentiator"** to frame competitive positioning.
- **"The moat"** for sustainable competitive advantage.
- Prefers **"ship"** over "deploy" or "launch" in engineering contexts.

---

## Calibration Examples

**Too formal:**
> *"The implementation of AI-driven forecasting systems represents a significant advancement in meteorological capability that warrants careful consideration of its strategic implications."*

**Right register:**
> *"AI forecasting is no longer experimental — NOAA just went operational. The baseline shifted. That changes what TWC's differentiation story needs to be."*

---

**Too hedged:**
> *"This could potentially be an interesting development that might have some relevance to how we think about product strategy going forward."*

**Right register:**
> *"This is worth watching. If agents can provision their own infrastructure, the product workflows we're building today assume a step that might not exist in two years."*
